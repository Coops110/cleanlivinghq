If you bought a cooling towel, used it on a muggy day and concluded it was a
gimmick, this page is for you. The product almost certainly worked exactly as
designed. It was just asked to work in conditions where the design has very
little to give.

## The mechanism only has one lever

Evaporative cooling works because turning liquid water into vapour requires a
large amount of energy — roughly 2,260 kilojoules per litre — and that energy
is taken from the nearest warm surface. That is why sweating cools you, and it
is the entire basis of a cooling towel.

The rate of evaporation depends on how much more water vapour the surrounding
air can absorb. Dry air has enormous spare capacity, so water leaves the fabric
fast and takes a lot of heat with it. Air that is already close to saturated
has very little spare capacity, so evaporation slows to a crawl and so does the
cooling.

Nothing about the towel changes this. A better towel holds more water; it
cannot make the air accept it.

## What this feels like in practice

At 20–40% relative humidity, an evaporative towel is genuinely impressive.
People who use them in dry heat tend to become evangelists about them.

At 60–80%, the effect is noticeably weaker. The towel still feels cool when you
first apply it — that is the water's temperature, not evaporation — and then
plateaus much sooner than you expect.

Above about 80% with still air, it does very little. The towel is wet and it
stays wet. This is also, not coincidentally, the condition in which your own
sweating stops working properly, which is why humid heat is more dangerous than
dry heat at the same temperature.

## The number that predicts it

Air temperature alone is a poor guide. The heat index combines temperature and
humidity into a single figure that reflects how the heat actually affects you,
and it is the number worth checking before a hot day's work.

of both your comfort and your safety than the thermometer on its own.

## What to do differently in humid heat

**Add airflow.** This is the single biggest thing you control. Moving air
carries saturated air away from the towel's surface and replaces it with
slightly drier air, which restarts evaporation. A breeze, a fan, or simply
walking makes a real difference. Snapping the towel does the same thing in
concentrated form, which is why it needs doing more often in humid conditions,
not less.

**Wring harder.** The instinct in extreme heat is to soak the towel. It is the
wrong instinct: water deep inside the fabric contributes nothing until it
reaches the surface. A properly wrung towel cools better than a dripping one.

**Re-wet more often, with cooler water.** In humidity you are getting more of
your benefit from conduction — the plain coldness of the water — and less from
evaporation. That shifts the balance toward frequent changes with genuinely
cool water.

**Cool on breaks, not just during work.** Actively cooling during a rest period
works better than passive rest in shade, and it is when you can apply water to
neck, wrists and forearms properly.

**Recognise when the answer is not a product.** In very humid heat, an
evaporative towel is a comfort measure and nothing more. Shade, airflow, fluid
and shorter work periods are what actually reduce risk. See
[working safely in heat](/heat-safety) for what genuinely prevents heat illness
and where cooling gear honestly sits in that list.

## The honest summary

A cooling towel is a good product with a clearly bounded operating envelope.
Sold as "instant cooling anywhere", it will disappoint half its buyers.
Understood as "an evaporation multiplier that works brilliantly in dry heat and
modestly in wet heat", it is a sensible purchase — and you will know in advance
which one you are going to get.
