Both materials cool by evaporation, and neither has any cooling ability of its
own — the water does the work. What separates them is how much water they hold
and how quickly they give it up, and that single difference changes which one
you should own.

## The short answer

**Buy PVA if you need cooling to last.** A full shift on site, a long ride, a
day in a show ring. It holds substantially more water per square inch and
releases it slowly, so the evaporation continues for longer between re-wets.

**Buy microfibre if you need it to be pleasant and portable.** A gym bag, a
running belt, a handbag in a heatwave. It stays soft, folds small, dries fast
and does not need reviving before you can use it.

If you are buying your first one and you work outdoors, PVA is the better
default. If you are buying one to keep in a bag for occasional use, microfibre
will annoy you less.

## What actually differs

| | PVA | Microfibre |
|---|---|---|
| Water held | High | Moderate |
| Cooling intensity | Stronger | Milder |
| Cooling duration | Longer | Shorter |
| Feel when damp | Slightly rubbery, smooth | Soft, cloth-like |
| Feel when dry | Rigid board | Soft, unchanged |
| Needs reviving before use | Yes, if fully dry | No |
| Packs small | Only while damp | Always |
| Typical use | Work, sport, sustained heat | Gym, travel, occasional |

The row that surprises people is "feel when dry". A PVA towel that has dried
out completely is genuinely hard — you cannot fold it, and a first-time buyer
often assumes it has been ruined. It has not. Thirty seconds under a tap brings
it straight back.

## Why PVA holds more water

Polyvinyl alcohol is processed into an open-cell structure, closer to a
synthetic sponge than to a woven cloth. That structure gives it far more
internal volume to hold water, and the cell walls release it gradually rather
than all at once.

Microfibre works differently. It traps water in the spaces between very fine
fibres by capillary action. That is excellent for mopping up water quickly —
which is why microfibre is good at drying things — but it also gives the water
up quickly, which is exactly what you do not want in a cooling towel.

This is the whole trade-off, and it explains every other difference in the
table. The property that makes microfibre a great drying cloth makes it a
mediocre cooling towel, and the property that makes PVA a great cooling towel
makes it a poor ordinary towel.

## Where humidity changes the answer

In humid heat, evaporation slows for both materials, and PVA's advantage
narrows. If the air cannot accept much more water vapour, holding more water
does not help you — you are limited by the air, not by the towel.

This is worth knowing before you spend more on PVA for a climate where it
will not get to show its advantage. If you routinely work in muggy heat, read
[why cooling towels underperform in humidity](/guides/why-cooling-towels-fail-in-humidity)
first, and check [the heat index](/tools/heat-index) for where you are — in
very high humidity, no evaporative product is the right answer and you need
shade, airflow and rest instead.

## What neither material does

Neither is antibacterial by default, and neither should be sold to you as
organic. Both are synthetic polymers. A cooling towel that spends its life
damp will grow mildew if you store it wet and forget about it — see
[how to clean and store a cooling towel](/guides/how-to-clean-a-cooling-towel)
for the routine that prevents it.

Neither material contains a coolant, a gel or a chemical that is "used up".
The cooling comes back every time you add water, for the life of the fabric.
Any product claiming a cooling agent that depletes is a different category of
product, and you should know which one you are buying.
