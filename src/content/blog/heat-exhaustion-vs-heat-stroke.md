If you work outdoors, this is the distinction worth knowing before you need it.
One condition is managed with rest and fluids. The other kills people, and
survival depends heavily on how fast cooling starts.

<div class="note note-vet">
<strong>If in doubt, treat it as heat stroke.</strong> Call 999 in the UK or 911
in the US and start cooling immediately. Over-reacting to heat exhaustion costs
an ambulance call. Under-reacting to heat stroke costs considerably more.
</div>

## The signs side by side

| | Heat exhaustion | Heat stroke |
|---|---|---|
| Mental state | Tired, irritable, headache — but lucid | Confused, slurring, aggressive, unconscious |
| Skin | Pale, cool, clammy | Hot, often flushed, may be dry |
| Sweating | Heavy | May continue, or may have stopped |
| Core temperature | Raised | Very high, typically above 40°C |
| Pulse | Fast, weak | Fast, strong |
| Other | Cramps, nausea, dizziness, thirst | Vomiting, seizure, collapse |
| Action | Stop, shade, cool, fluids, monitor | **Emergency call now**, cool aggressively |

## The one row that matters most

**Mental state.** Everything else on that table has exceptions. Someone can
have heat stroke and still be sweating. Skin can be misleading. Temperature is
rarely measured on site.

But a change in how someone is thinking or behaving — repeating themselves,
becoming uncharacteristically irritable, stumbling, going quiet, not making
sense — is the signal that the body's thermoregulation has failed and the brain
is affected. That is heat stroke until a doctor says otherwise.

This is also why the buddy check works and self-monitoring does not. The person
affected is, by definition, the one least able to assess it.

## First five minutes: heat exhaustion

1. Stop work. Not "ease off" — stop.
2. Get into genuine shade, ideally somewhere with moving air.
3. Remove PPE and loosen clothing.
4. Cool actively: cold water on skin, a wet cloth or
   [cooling towel](/cooling-towels) at the neck, a fan.
5. Sip fluids steadily. Water is fine; over several hours of heavy sweating,
   something with electrolytes is better.
6. Watch them. Improvement should be clear within 30 minutes.

If they are not clearly improving in 30 minutes, or they get worse at any point,
escalate to the emergency response below.

## First five minutes: suspected heat stroke

1. **Call emergency services immediately.** Do this first, or have someone else
   do it while you start step 2.
2. Move them to shade.
3. Strip outer clothing and PPE.
4. **Cool aggressively.** Cold water over as much skin as possible — dousing,
   a hose, wet sheets. Ice packs to neck, armpits and groin. Fan them hard.
   Where it is available and safe, whole-body immersion in cold water is the
   most effective method there is.
5. If they are unconscious and breathing, put them in the recovery position.
6. Do not try to give fluids to anyone who is confused or not fully conscious.
7. Keep cooling until the ambulance arrives. Do not stop because they seem
   better.

The phrase used in sports medicine is "cool first, transport second". Time at a
dangerous core temperature is what causes the damage, and cooling started on
site saves more than a faster drive.

## Who is most at risk

Not who people expect. The highest-risk group on any site is **new starters and
people returning from leave**, because acclimatisation takes one to two weeks
of graded exposure and is lost after about a week away. A fit, experienced
worker back from a fortnight's holiday into a heatwave is a textbook case.

Also raised risk: anyone unwell or hungover, anyone on medication affecting
sweating or fluid balance, anyone doing unusually heavy work, and anyone in
impermeable PPE — which blocks evaporation completely and removes your main
cooling route.

## Preventing it in the first place

Recognition is the last line. What actually keeps people out of trouble is
acclimatisation, shade, steady fluids, sensible scheduling and watching each
other. [Working safely in heat](/heat-safety) covers all of it in order of how
much difference each one makes — and it is honest about where cooling gear
sits in that list, which is near the bottom.

To know what conditions you are actually facing, the
[heat risk calculator](/tools/heat-index) turns a temperature and humidity
reading into a risk band.
