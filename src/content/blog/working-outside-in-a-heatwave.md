Written for people who cannot simply go indoors. This is the practical layer on
top of [working safely in heat](/heat-safety) — the checklist version.

## The night before

- **Hydrate then, not in the morning.** Arriving already short is the most
  common avoidable mistake, and you cannot catch up during the shift.
- **Go easy on alcohol.** It is a diuretic and it degrades sleep, and poor sleep
  measurably reduces heat tolerance.
- **Freeze what you can.** Half-fill bottles and freeze them overnight, then top
  up in the morning — you get cold water for hours instead of minutes.
- **Check tomorrow's numbers.** Not just the temperature. Humidity is half the
  story; run them through the [heat risk calculator](/tools/heat-index).

## Start of shift

- **Front-load the hard work.** Moving the heaviest tasks into the first hours
  removes more risk than any equipment.
- **Agree the buddy pairs.** Everyone watching one specific other person, and
  knowing what they are watching for.
- **Confirm where the shade and water actually are.** Not in principle — where.
- **Flag anyone new or just back.** First few days and post-leave returns are
  the highest-risk people on site, every time.
- **Wet your cooling towel now.** It takes a minute and it is far easier than
  doing it when you are already struggling.

## Every hour

- **Drink about a cup every 15 to 20 minutes.** Steadily. Not a litre at once
  on a break.
- **Take the break in shade, and cool actively during it.** Active cooling
  during rest gets your core temperature down faster than sitting still does,
  which makes the same break worth more.
- **Look at your buddy properly.** Are they making sense? Same as an hour ago?
- **Re-wet and re-snap the towel.** In humid conditions it needs it more often,
  not less — see
  [why cooling towels underperform in humidity](/guides/why-cooling-towels-fail-in-humidity).
- **Check your urine colour.** Crude, unglamorous and genuinely the most
  practical hydration check available on a site.

## Clothing and PPE

- **Light colours, loose weave, long sleeves.** Counterintuitively, covering up
  in loose light fabric beats bare skin in strong sun: you avoid the radiant
  load and burning while still allowing evaporation.
- **Know what your PPE costs you.** Impermeable coveralls block evaporation
  entirely, which removes your primary cooling route. If you are in them, your
  work-rest cycle needs to change, not just your water intake.
- **Wide brim over a cap.** Ears and the back of the neck are where outdoor
  workers burn.
- **A damp cooling towel under a hard hat harness or inside a collar.** This is
  where the product genuinely earns its place — see
  [how cooling towels work](/cooling-towels) for why the neck specifically.

## Signs to stop immediately

Any of these means stop, shade, cool, and get help:

- Confusion, or behaving unlike themselves
- Stopping sweating in the heat
- Vomiting
- Fainting or near-fainting
- A headache that does not ease with rest and water

The full picture, and the difference between the two conditions, is in
[heat exhaustion or heat stroke](/guides/heat-exhaustion-vs-heat-stroke).

## After the shift

- **Keep drinking.** Rehydration continues for hours after you stop.
- **Eat something salty** if you have been sweating hard all day.
- **Cool your sleeping space** if you can. Consecutive hot nights are what turns
  a manageable heatwave into a dangerous one, because you never fully recover.
- **Rinse the cooling towel** rather than leaving it in the bag —
  [how to clean and store one](/guides/how-to-clean-a-cooling-towel).
