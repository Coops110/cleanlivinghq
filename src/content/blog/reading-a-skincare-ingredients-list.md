The front of a jar is marketing. The ingredients list is a regulated document,
and once you know how to read it, it tells you most of what you need.

## The list is ordered, and that is the useful part

Ingredients appear in **descending order by weight**, down to one percent. That
one rule does more work than anything else on the label.

If water — *Aqua* — is first, the product is mostly water. That is not an
insult; most creams and lotions are, and water is what makes them light and
spreadable. But it does mean the "with argan oil" on the front deserves a look
at where argan oil actually sits.

Below one percent, order is no longer required. So a much-advertised active
sitting near the end of a long list may be present at a token concentration —
enough to name it on the front, not necessarily enough to do anything. This is
common, legal and worth spotting.

## INCI names are a feature, not obfuscation

The list uses INCI names: *Butyrospermum Parkii* rather than shea butter,
*Cera Alba* rather than beeswax, *Tocopherol* rather than vitamin E.

It reads like an attempt to hide something. It is the opposite. A standardised
international naming system means the same ingredient carries the same name in
every market, so you can compare two products from different countries directly,
and someone with an allergy can reliably identify what they must avoid.

A few worth recognising:

| INCI name | What it is |
|---|---|
| Aqua | Water |
| Butyrospermum Parkii | Shea butter |
| Cera Alba | Beeswax |
| Cocos Nucifera | Coconut |
| Helianthus Annuus | Sunflower |
| Tocopherol | Vitamin E |
| Parfum / Fragrance | Fragrance blend, composition not disclosed |
| Limonene, Linalool, Citral | Fragrance components that must be declared as allergens |

That last row is worth understanding. Those names appearing at the end of a
list usually does not mean synthetic fragrance has been added — they occur
naturally in essential oils, and they are declared because they are recognised
allergens. A product with a long tail of them is often *more* botanical, not
less.

## Balms have short lists for a structural reason

An anhydrous product — one containing no water — does not need a preservative
system, because the microbes that spoil cosmetics need water to grow. That is
why a balm can genuinely have four ingredients and a cream cannot.

It also means a "preservative-free" claim on a cream deserves a question, and
the same claim on a balm is simply a description of the chemistry. Neither is
better; they are different formats for different jobs.

## Claims that mean something, and claims that do not

**Means something:**

- A named certifier: COSMOS, Soil Association, Ecocert, USDA Organic, along
  with a stated percentage of organic content.
- A specific concentration: "10% niacinamide".
- "Fragrance-free" — a defined term, distinct from "unscented".

**Means little on its own:**

- "Natural". Not meaningfully regulated for cosmetics.
- "Chemical-free". Everything is a chemical, water included.
- "Dermatologically tested". Tested is not the same as passed, and no threshold
  is implied.
- "Unscented". Can mean a masking fragrance was added to cover a raw material's
  smell.
- "Non-toxic". No agreed definition.
- "Clean". A marketing category, not a standard.
